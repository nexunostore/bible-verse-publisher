Question: how can john chapter 11 verse 25 help someone who has lost a mother.
============================================================

“I am the resurrection and the life. The one who believes in me will live, even though they die.”  
— John 11:25

Losing a mother is one of the deepest wounds a human heart can know. It is not just the loss of a person, but the loss of the one whose presence helped define our world: her voice, her prayers, her quiet sacrifices, her love that was just “there.” When death comes that close, Bible verses can sometimes feel like thin paper laid over a chasm.

And yet, John 11:25 was spoken into a scene of raw, fresh grief. It was not delivered in a calm classroom, but at a graveside, to a woman who had just buried her brother and could barely see past the ache in her chest. That is precisely why this verse can become a lifeline for someone who has lost a mother.

In this post, we’ll look at how John 11:25 can meet you in your loss—honestly, not superficially—through four themes:

1. Jesus meets us *in* grief, not around it  
2. Jesus redefines death from the inside  
3. Jesus offers a hope that is more than “she’s in a better place”  
4. Jesus invites you into a new way of living with your mother’s memory

---

## 1. Jesus meets us *in* grief, not around it

John 11 tells the story of Lazarus, the brother of Mary and Martha. By the time Jesus arrives in Bethany, Lazarus has been dead and buried for four days. The house is full of mourners; the air is thick with weeping and the rituals of grief. Culturally, this was the week of intense mourning when family would sit low to the ground, friends would bring food, and visitors would come simply to “sit in the sadness” with them.

Into that setting, Martha runs out to meet Jesus and says words that anyone who has lost a loved one can understand:  

“Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died” (John 11:21).

It’s half confession of faith (“You could have stopped this”) and half cry of disappointment (“But you didn’t”). Many who lose a mother feel something similar, even if they’re afraid to say it out loud: “Lord, if you had stepped in… if you had healed… if You had just done something.”

Notice what Jesus does *not* do.

- He does not rebuke Martha for saying the wrong thing.
- He does not rush to correct her emotions.
- He does not tell her, “You shouldn’t be this sad—you’ll see him again.”

Instead, He stays in the moment with her, then gently redirects her gaze:  

“Your brother will rise again.” (v. 23)

Martha answers with standard Jewish belief: “I know he will rise again in the resurrection at the last day” (v. 24). It’s as if she says, “Yes, I know the doctrine. I know the right answer. But that doesn’t touch what I feel *today*.”

This is where Jesus makes His astonishing claim:  

“I am the resurrection and the life. The one who believes in me will live, even though they die;  
and whoever lives by believing in me will never die. Do you believe this?” (vv. 25–26)

Before He ever raises Lazarus, before He ever fixes the situation, He does something crucial: He makes Himself present in Martha’s grief and speaks to *her* heart.

That means this verse is not a “verse to shut grief down.” It’s a verse Jesus spoke *into* grief:

- He *saw* the sorrow: John says He was “deeply moved” and that “Jesus wept” at the tomb. Commentators note that “deeply moved” carries a sense of anger, even righteous rage, at the ravages of death and the pain it causes.
- He *honored* the relationship: John underlines that Jesus loved Lazarus and his sisters. Your love for your mother, and your pain in losing her, are not embarrassments to hide; they are what love looks like in a fallen world.

If you have lost your mother, this is the first comfort of John 11:25:  
Jesus does not stand distant with a theological clipboard. He comes close, looks your grief in the face, and speaks from within it.

---

## 2. Jesus redefines death from the inside

Standing in front of Lazarus’s tomb, Jesus doesn’t only promise “resurrection someday.” He says, “*I am* the resurrection and the life.”

That shift—from “something that will happen” to “someone who is here”—is profound.

### Death as Jesus sees it

Throughout Scripture, death is real and terrible, but it is not ultimate:

- It is often called an “enemy” (1 Cor. 15:26), not a friend.
- For believers, it’s portrayed as “sleep,” not because we are unconscious in some empty void, but because from God’s perspective it has become a temporary state from which He can and will awaken His people (1 Thess. 4:13–14; John 11:11–14).

In John 11 specifically:

- Lazarus is in the tomb, four days dead. His body has begun to decay. The grief is not hypothetical; it is physical, ugly, and final in every earthly sense.
- Yet Jesus, by His word alone, calls Lazarus out of the grave. Lazarus’s return is not the final resurrection (he will die again someday), but it is a sign—a preview—of what Jesus will ultimately do for all who belong to Him.

Jesus’ own later resurrection turns that preview into a guarantee. As several of your books note, the New Testament treats His resurrection as the “firstfruits” of a coming harvest. What happened to Him bodily is the pattern and pledge of what will happen to His people.

So when Jesus says, “I am the resurrection and the life,” He is saying two interwoven things:

1. **He Himself has power over death.**  
   He doesn’t merely teach about life; He *gives* it. He can stand outside a grave and reverse what no human being can undo.

2. **Union with Him changes what death means for believers.**  
   “The one who believes in me will live, even though they die.” Physical death remains real—we still stand at hospital beds and gravesides—but for the believer, death becomes a passageway, not a wall. As one commentator put it, for the Christian, dying is more like moving than disappearing.

If your mother trusted in Christ, then John 11:25 says this about her:

- She died, but she still lives.  
- God will one day raise her body, restored and glorified, as surely as He raised His Son.  
- Death has not erased her; it has relocated her.

If you’re unsure where your mother stood spiritually, this verse still speaks a crucial truth:  
Jesus is Lord over death itself. The One who will judge with perfect justice is also the One who wept at a grave and bore death in His own body. Whatever questions you carry about your mother’s story, they are held in the hands of Someone who is both perfectly righteous and deeply compassionate.

---

## 3. A hope that is more than “she’s in a better place”

In times of loss, we reach instinctively for sentences that might dull the pain:  

“She’s in a better place.”  
“At least she’s not suffering.”  
“She’ll always be with you in your heart.”

These can be sincere attempts at comfort, but they often feel thin. They don’t deal fully with the outrage that death exists at all. They don’t answer the ache for *this* mother, *this* voice, *this* body, in *this* real world.

John 11:25 offers something thicker, weightier:

> “The one who believes in me will live, even though they die;  
> and whoever lives by believing in me will never die.”

Two layers of hope flow from that.

### 1. The hope of reunion

If your mother belonged to Christ by faith, then Scripture holds out a clear promise:

- She is currently “with the Lord” in a state far better than anything this life could offer (Phil. 1:23).
- She will be raised bodily when Christ returns, in a world where “there will no longer be any death; there will no longer be any mourning, or crying, or pain” (Rev. 21:4).
- That means your separation, as managing as it feels now, is temporary. Your grief is real, but it is not the last word.

The New Testament often speaks of this hope specifically to comfort grieving believers. Paul tells the Thessalonians not to “sorrow as others who have no hope,” not because sorrow is wrong but because Christian sorrow is shaped like a comma, not a period. It’s like saying goodbye at an airport, knowing that the separation—however long and painful—is not permanent.

### 2. The hope of cosmic restoration

John 11 also plugs your personal grief into a much bigger story.

When Jesus says, “I am the resurrection and the life,” He is not only talking about individual survival after death. As biblical theologians emphasize, the resurrection is part of God’s plan to renew *all creation*: a renewed earth, renewed bodies, a world suffused with God’s presence.

That means:

- The God who watched your mother suffer and die is also the God who will one day make right everything death has stolen.
- Your mother’s story, if she was in Christ, is bound up with the story of a world made new. Her future is not to float forever as a disembodied soul, but to share in Christ’s own resurrection-life in a renewed creation.

So Christian comfort is not: “Your mother lives on in your memories” or “Her love is in the clouds.”  
Christian comfort is: “Your mother, if she died in Christ, is secure in His presence now, and He will one day raise her to embodied, joyful life in a remade world where death cannot follow.”

That’s a hope big enough to stand under real grief.

---

## 4. Living with grief in the light of John 11:25

Knowing these truths doesn’t erase the ache of missing your mother. It does, however, begin to shape *how* you live with that ache.

Here are some ways John 11:25 can practically help someone who has lost a mother.

### a) It validates your grief instead of shaming it

Because Jesus Himself wept at a grave—knowing full well that He was about to raise Lazarus—your tears are not a sign of weak faith. They are part of love.

The Bible’s own language for grief is strong and honest. As one dictionary notes, words for grief in Scripture often highlight the *cause*—the brokenness of the world—more than they analyze the emotion. To grieve is to feel the shock of a fallen creation colliding with the goodness of love.

So:

- You don’t have to rush yourself toward “closure.”  
- You don’t have to pretend you’re “fine” because you believe in heaven.  
- You are free to say what Martha said: “Lord, if You had been here…”—and then to stay long enough in His presence to hear Him say, “I am the resurrection and the life.”

### b) It invites you to cling to a Person, not just to a doctrine

When a mother dies, people may throw verses at you like bandages. Sometimes those verses are absolutely true and utterly badly timed.

John 11:25 models something different. Jesus does give Martha truth—but He gives Himself with it:

> “I am the resurrection and the life… Do you believe this?” (v. 26)

Faith here is not merely “Do you assent to this doctrine?” but “Will you entrust yourself to Me in the midst of this?”

In seasons of grief, your faith may feel fragile, filled more with questions than confidence. But the power of this promise doesn’t rest on the intensity of your faith; it rests on the faithfulness of the One you’re clinging to.

You can pray, simply and honestly:

- “Jesus, You say You are the resurrection and the life. I don’t always feel that right now. Help me trust You with my mother. Help me trust You with my future.”
- “Hold me where I cannot hold myself.”

### c) It reorients your relationship to your mother’s memory

Grief often brings:

- Regrets (“I wish I’d said…”)  
- Longing (“I just want one more conversation”)  
- Fear (“Will I forget her voice? Her laugh?”)

John 11:25 doesn’t make those vanish, but it reframes them.

If your mother is in Christ:

- You are not simply “remembering someone who is gone.” You are honoring someone who is alive to God and will one day be raised.
- Your memories become, in a sense, seeds of hope—fragments of a story that God has not finished writing.

This can gently transform the way you think and write about her:

- You can give thanks for the ways she imaged Christ to you—her sacrifices, her tenderness, even her imperfect attempts to love you.
- You can allow your longing for her to become also a longing for Christ’s appearing, when all such separations will end.

### d) It helps you face your own mortality without despair

Losing a mother often forces you, perhaps for the first time, to confront your own death. You are now one generation closer to the front line. The “veil” feels thinner.

Into that anxiety, John 11:25 speaks *personally* to you:

> “The one who believes in me will live, even though they die.”

Death will still come, unless Christ returns first. But if you are united to Him by faith, it cannot sever you from God. Your spirit now, and your body one day, are destined for resurrection.

Far from making you morbid, this can:

- Free you from panic about aging and sickness.
- Loosen the grip of lesser fears and trivial pursuits.
- Lead you to live more fully, knowing that this life—beautiful and painful as it is—is not all you get.

---

## Bringing it together: a graveside promise for a child of a mother

John 11 ends with Lazarus walking out of the tomb, graveclothes still wrapped around him, and Jesus saying, “Take off the grave clothes and let him go” (v. 44). That scene is “a sign,” not the finish line. Lazarus will die again. But for those watching, it’s a breakthrough in the dark—a crack in the seeming finality of death.

For someone who has lost a mother, John 11:25 is like that breakthrough:

- It does not deny the tomb. It stands right in front of it.  
- It does not deny the tears. Jesus’ own cheeks are wet.  
- It does not offer vague comfort. It names a Person: “I am the resurrection and the life.”

If your mother trusted Christ, then:

- She is now in His care, beyond the reach of pain and decay.
- He will one day call her name as surely as He called Lazarus’s.
- The embrace you miss is not lost forever; it is postponed until the day when death is finally destroyed.

If you are not sure where she stood, or if your own faith feels shaky, this same Jesus stands before *you* and asks, as He asked Martha: “Do you believe this?”

Not, “Have you got all your questions answered?”  
Not, “Are you strong enough to never doubt again?”  

But: “Will you bring your grief, your anger, your confusion, and place them in My hands? Will you trust Me—not only with your eternity, but with your mother’s story and your own broken heart?”

John 11:25 is not a slogan for a sympathy card. It is a graveside declaration from a Savior who hates death enough to go through it, and who loves you enough to meet you in its shadow. If you let it, this promise can become not just a verse you know, but a lifeline you hold as you walk through life without your mother’s earthly presence—and toward the day when, in Christ, you and she will stand together in a world where funerals are no more.
